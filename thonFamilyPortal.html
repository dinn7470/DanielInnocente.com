A portal that tells families what to do next.

THON families juggle onboarding, event registration, and a weekly flood of updates. I helped redesign their portal so the next step is always the first thing they see.

Stakeholder THON Family Relations Director, THON Families

Overview

Why THON asked for a redesign

The Family Relations Director asked THON Technology to rethink how families receive updates, complete onboarding, and stay connected throughout the year.

Product context

The portal helps THON families complete onboarding, update information, and keep track of events.

UX focus

Families needed a calmer place to understand what mattered now, not another dense update channel.

Design outcome

The redesign prioritizes event planning, clearer next steps, and a warmer connection to THON.

Problem

Families had to work too hard to find what mattered.

The portal and weekly updates contained useful information, but the experience did not consistently organize that information around family decisions.

01

Find the action

Updating information, registering for events, and managing THON Weekend details needed to feel like a clear path, not disconnected tasks.

02

Understand events

Families needed to quickly scan event type, timing, relevance, and next steps without relying on long weekly updates.

03

Feel connected

The interface needed to feel like a community touchpoint, not just a place to submit sensitive family information.

Research findings

What Family Relations Chairs told us

Aiden Coleman and I interviewed 5 Family Relations Chairs from partner organizations. We couldn't interview THON families directly for privacy reasons, so we went to the people who support them every week. We asked what families ask for, where confusion happens, and how paired organizations build relationships with new families.

01

Families need a clear next step

Information is most helpful when it is tied to a visible action, deadline, or event.

02

Connection starts before the first meeting

Optional family interests and hobbies could help paired organizations break the ice with care.

03

Concise beats comprehensive

The portal needed to summarize the weekly email experience, not recreate its density.

Before and after

The calendar moved from a small preview to a real planning tool.

The dashboard below handled registration well, but it treated events as a sidebar afterthought. I reframed the calendar as its own destination, then added an events page so discovery and sign-ups had somewhere to live.

Before · Dashboard

The dashboard oriented families, but events had nowhere to live.

  • The calendar was a sidebar preview: families could see that dates existed, but the panel was too narrow to read an event, compare weeks, or plan around anything.
  • No way to browse what was coming: a family could tell something was scheduled without being able to open it, find out what it was, or sign up from the same place.
  • Registration was solved, discovery was not: the action cards handled forms well, but nothing on the page helped a family decide which events they actually wanted to attend.
After · Events

Discovery got its own page, built as an experience rather than a list.

  • Photo-rich event cards: each event leads with real imagery from THON activities, making the page feel like a community space rather than a spreadsheet of dates.
  • Contextual CTAs per event: families see VIEW, SIGN-UP, or ATTENDING depending on their status, so every card has an obvious next action rather than requiring navigation elsewhere.
  • Upcoming vs. past separated clearly: the page distinguishes what's actionable now from what has passed, so families are never unsure whether a deadline is still open.
After · Calendar

The sidebar preview became a full calendar families can plan in.

  • Three event categories, color coded: THON Wide, Org Related, and Personal events each get a distinct color and a toggleable visibility control, letting families filter down to what's relevant to them.
  • Click to expand event detail: selecting a date opens an inline detail card with the event name, date range, and quick context, with no new page required to check what something is.
  • Families can add personal events: the "Add New Event" button lets families layer in their own dates alongside THON events, making the calendar a genuine planning tool rather than a read-only feed.

Design decisions

Four decisions shaped the redesign

Separate the task from the journey

Updating family info is a task. Staying connected to THON is the journey. The redesign gives each its own space so neither crowds the other out.

Surface actions, not fields

Every page leads with what a family can do (register, view, update, or add) rather than presenting information and expecting families to figure out the next step.

Let the community speak

Photography from real THON events replaces blank form space, making the portal feel like an entry point into the THON world, not a bureaucratic requirement.

Build in relationship starters

Optional interests and hobbies fields give paired organizations something personal to work with when reaching out to families for the first time.

What this shows

What this project taught me

The portal's practical job is registrations and updates. Its emotional job is making families feel remembered inside a huge organization, and the redesign had to do both at once. It also taught me a research lesson: when you can't reach your users directly, the people who support them every week are the next-best source of truth.

The concept was handed off to the incoming design team when I graduated and hasn't shipped yet. That's a scheduling reality rather than a verdict on the work. THON's design and development run on the academic year, and while the Family Relations Director commissioned the redesign, building it requires THON Technology to prioritize it in their queue. A handoff at graduation also means the incoming team has to validate and re-own the work before they commit to building it. That's the normal shape of a student organization project, not a rejection.

If I could keep going, I'd close the research gap first. Interviewing chairs instead of families was this project's biggest limitation, so the next phase would be moderated usability testing with three to five THON families on the Figma prototype, aimed at one question: does the dashboard's task framing, "here's what you need to do next," actually match how families think about their obligations? I'd also work with THON Technology to identify the single highest-leverage screen to ship first, probably the dashboard, so progress doesn't depend on rebuilding everything at once.

There's a trade-off worth naming explicitly. I spent the majority of the project on the calendar and events experience because that's where the research pointed, but I never touched the onboarding flow, which is the first thing a new family sees. That was a deliberate scope call, and it means the redesign solves the steady-state experience and leaves the first impression untouched. The uncomfortable conversation I kept having with myself was about the proxy research: it wasn't just a limitation, it was a methodological risk. Chairs described what families asked them about, but a person summarizing someone else's confusion is not the same as watching that confusion happen live. I made peace with it as the best available option, and I'd still push much harder for direct family access in any future version of this project, even if that meant a smaller sample.
