Bringing real users into an AI-built job tool.

Arbiter's interface was generated with AI before I arrived. It scanned a resume, estimated your career level, and recommended roles, but nobody had asked job seekers what they'd do with any of that. My internship was about closing that gap.

Overview

Why this product needed a designer

ConvoRecs had a strong technical concept, and AI generation got a working interface in front of people fast. What it couldn't generate was an understanding of the users. My job was to supply the missing half: talk to the students Arbiter was built for, find out where the experience lost them, and redesign it around their answers.

Product context

Arbiter analyzes a resume, estimates a user's career level, and recommends roles that may fit.

UX focus

I studied how users interpreted the AI output, where trust broke down, and what controls they expected.

Design outcome

The redesign makes recommendations easier to refine and reframes results around action, not just analysis.

Problem

Users got answers. They didn't know what to do with them.

The AI's output was often accurate. But accuracy isn't usefulness: the interface dumped analysis on users without organizing it around the decisions in front of them.

01

Interpret the result

Resume analysis, career level, agent debate, and job matches competed for attention, making the main takeaway hard to find.

02

Trust the reasoning

The system showed how it worked, but the explanation could feel more like a technical log than guidance for a job seeker.

03

Refine the match

Users needed obvious ways to adjust recommendations by location, compensation, experience level, role type, and other real constraints.

04

Move forward

Career level was useful only if users could understand why they were placed there and what would help them progress.

Before / after

The redesign turns AI output into decisions users can adjust.

The original product already had a strong technical idea. My redesign focused the experience around the decisions users needed to make: what constraints matter, what level they are, and what to do next.

Before

Results competed with the next step.

  • Issue: The interface presented a lot of signal at once without clearly prioritizing user decisions.
  • Effect: Refinement felt secondary, even though it was central to improving match quality.
After

Refinement became a control surface.

  • Choice: Turn vague feedback into visible controls that map to job-search decisions.
  • Why: The AI output becomes something users can steer instead of something they only receive.
Before

Personalization came too late.

  • Issue: Real constraints like location, compensation, and role type were not part of setup.
  • Effect: Users had to wait for results before seeing whether recommendations matched their situation.
After

Filters moved before the scan.

  • Choice: Add plain-language filters before analysis so users can shape recommendations around real limits.
  • Why: Earlier control makes the AI feel more useful, predictable, and accountable.

Process detail

Keep transparency visible, but do not let it overpower the user's goal.

I treated the agent pipeline as supporting context: helpful for trust, but less important than the user's level, fit, and next action. That framing keeps the AI explainable without making the page feel like a technical log.

Research findings

What job seekers told me

I ran 5 interviews with college students who were actively applying to jobs and internships (Arbiter's exact target users), asking how they search, what they distrust, and what they'd want an AI tool to explain. Three findings shaped every redesign decision below.

Finding 01

Users wanted reasons, not just recommendations.

A job match only felt useful when users could understand why it fit, what evidence the AI used, and what tradeoffs were behind the recommendation.

Finding 02

Career level needed context.

A level label helped only when users could see why they were placed there and what would help them move toward stronger roles.

Finding 03

Control changed trust.

Users were more willing to trust AI feedback when they could adjust constraints like location, compensation, experience level, and role type.

Usability testing

Then I watched five people actually use it.

Interviews told me what job seekers wanted. Usability sessions told me where the build still lost them. I brought the same five participants back for moderated sessions, logged 40 distinct findings, and grouped them into nine themes so the team could separate one person's preference from the problems everybody hit. Participants are anonymized as P1 to P5.

Agent conversation

All 5 participants
  • Collapse the agent dialogue or summarize it. It should not be expanded by default.
  • "AI Agents" is the wrong label: several participants did not know what an agent was.
  • Each agent needs a plain-language description of what it actually does.
  • Split opinion: some participants would read the full exchange, others never would.

Level classification

All 5 participants
  • Participants did not understand what the classification terms meant.
  • The word "level" read as skill level to some and career level to others.
  • Levels need defining, with a public benchmark like levels.fyi for reference.
  • The explanation for why a level won was too long, vague, and repetitive.
  • Classification terms could be explained during the loading state.

Visual design and UI

All 5 participants
  • The colored outer border looked, in a participant's words, obviously AI-generated.
  • Emojis split the room: a useful agent indicator to some, an AI tell to others.
  • Progress and percentage bars needed more visual weight to be readable at a glance.
  • Requests for a grid layout for job results and a side-by-side comparison view.

Scrolling and layout

P1, P3, P4
  • The results container should stay fixed instead of moving as new content loads.
  • Do not auto-scroll users to the bottom when the page updates.
  • Results belong at the top of the page.
  • Live updates flashed at the top on refresh.

Job search results

P1, P3, P4
  • Show one stage of the search at a time and make the timeline explicit.
  • One participant was recommended their own current job; another hit a dead link.
  • "Active" was undefined as a status, and end dates were missed entirely.
  • Participants wanted more roles per level and control over how many appear.

Filters

P1, P3
  • Location, compensation, work style, and company type belong on the home page.
  • Filters should behave universally, the way they do on LinkedIn.

Evidence and quotes

P1, P2
  • The "evidence from search" section read as redundant; the reasoning mattered more.
  • Participants could not tell where the quotes were sourced from.

Career trajectory

P3, P4, P5
  • Participants liked seeing where a career could go next.
  • They wanted a job outlook summary rather than raw agent conversation.
  • One participant did not care about trajectory information at all.

What already worked

P1, P3, P5
  • The breakdown shown while the resume processes was well received.
  • The job summary read clearly, and open-by-default tabs were appreciated.
  • "Refine your results" landed, which confirmed the direction of the redesign.

What testing changed

Three findings went straight into the redesign. Filters moved to the entry point because participants asked for them there before analysis ran. The agent conversation was demoted from the centerpiece to supporting evidence, because every participant wanted it collapsed. The FAQ page exists because five out of five people could not explain what an AI agent was or what their level meant. The colored border came off for the same reason a participant flagged it: it made a real product look generated.

Career pathway

The redesign also reframes recommendations as forward motion.

Beyond improving the scan flow, I explored how Arbiter could help users understand how their current level connects to the roles they want next.

  • Choice: Shift from a static score to a clearer view of where the user stands.
  • Why: Recruiters and job seekers both need the product to communicate growth, not just classification.
  • Result: The concept supports a more guided career-planning experience without adding more noise to the scan page.

FAQ page

Designing clarity around the AI so users don't get lost in the output.

Users kept asking the same questions during testing: What even is an AI Agent? and What do these job levels actually mean? I proposed and designed a FAQ page accessible from the top nav that answers these upfront, in plain language.

Solution

What changed in the redesign

Make the AI easier to steer

Custom filters and plain-language refinement so users can shape recommendations around their real constraints.

Separate signal from system noise

Keep agent debate visible, but prioritize the user's final level, role fit, and recommended next steps.

Explain career level in context

Help users understand whether a role is a best fit, stretch goal, or future pathway. Not all results are equivalent.

Connect jobs to career progress

Use outputs to show what skills or experiences could move the user toward the roles they want next.

What this shows

What this project taught me

An AI can generate a plausible interface in an afternoon. It can't sit across from a stressed-out senior three weeks from graduation and hear what "career level" means to them. That gap is the job. Arbiter is where I learned that designing for AI products mostly means designing for the person the AI can't see.

The redesign was a deliverable, not a deployment. Whether ConvoRecs builds it is their product and engineering decision, and my internship ended before that call was made. That's the normal shape of internship work: the output is a research-backed recommendation, not a pull request. The company still owns the AI-generated interface and would need to prioritize the rebuild in their own cycle.

The redesign changed how the interface presents information, but it never touched the matching algorithm underneath. Students kept asking why a specific job was recommended to them, and that transparency gap is the next design problem. If I kept going, I'd prototype an explanation layer on the job card ("matched because: your GPA, your stated interest in operations") and test whether seeing the reasoning increases how often students actually apply. I'd also push for an A/B test of the redesign against the original, because interviews tell me what people say and comparative data tells me what they do.

That scope was a trade-off I made deliberately. Leaving the algorithm alone was the right call for an internship, because the algorithm belongs to ConvoRecs, but it meant I was redesigning the packaging around a black box. I also took a wrong turn in sequencing: I ran the heuristic evaluation before the user interviews, so I spent time identifying problems the interface had in principle before I knew which ones actually cost users anything. The other order would have made the evaluation sharper. If I were starting over, I'd push harder on sample diversity too. All five participants were Penn State students, which is Arbiter's target user, but they shared a campus culture that probably narrowed the range of mental models I was testing against.
