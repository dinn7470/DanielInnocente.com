Overview

Where this project came from

SupportMe began as a research paper and Figma prototype with Penn State University's ReSENSE Research Lab in 2025.

User context

Many seniors face illness, financial strain, isolation, and digital tools that assume confidence and strong social networks.

UX focus

The design needed to support asking for help without forcing people into public performance or oversharing.

Design outcome

The concept combines peer support, needs-based fundraising, and small Community Fund requests in one accessible space.

Problem

The existing support model often rewards people who already have reach.

I synthesized 35+ peer-reviewed studies, platform reports, and demographic datasets. Primary research with seniors wasn't feasible, so the literature served as my user data. It shows seniors are disadvantaged before they ever press "publish."

Visibility depends on popularity

Crowdfunding platforms often reward campaigns that already have engagement, which can bury seniors with smaller networks.

Only about 10% of campaigns reach full funding. Routine needs like prescription refills succeed at 8%, while dramatic medical cases succeed at roughly 45%.

Storytelling becomes labor

Success often depends on writing an emotional, polished story. That can pressure people to overshare during vulnerable moments.

One campaigner refused to share his insulin fundraiser publicly: "my pride's worth more than that to me."

Social reach is treated like access

Campaigns rely heavily on social sharing, disadvantaging older adults who are less active or less confident online.

Returning creators succeed at nearly double the site average, and 73% of older adults report needing technical help just setting up new devices.

Design decisions

Every research finding became a product decision.

01

Rotate visibility

Requests are surfaced through fair rotation instead of likes, comments, or shares.

02

Ask for need, not performance

The request flow focuses on clear context and practical need, not dramatic storytelling.

03

Build support inside the product

Discovery, forums, and Community Fund requests work without depending on outside social media.

04

Make accessibility visible

Readable type, simple navigation, and easy to find controls are treated as core features.

05

Protect trust

Privacy forward patterns reduce fear around sharing health or financial information.

Prototype

The interface stays warm without hiding the serious parts.

Product model

SupportMe blends emotional care with practical help.

Peer communities

Condition and life stage groups for encouragement, shared experience, and belonging.

Fair discovery

Requests rotate through the platform instead of being ranked by popularity.

Community Fund

Micro assistance for urgent needs like prescriptions, groceries, co-pays, or rides.

Fit Me controls

Readable, adjustable, senior friendly interface settings placed where people need them.

Validation plan

The study I designed to test it

The paper defines the evaluation protocol for the next stage: semi-guided walkthroughs with participants aged 50+, measuring task completion, time, hesitations, and mis-clicks across the core flows, plus a dedicated accessibility evaluation with low-vision and hard-of-hearing participants. The questions it's built to answer:

  • Can seniors complete onboarding, posting, and request flows with confidence?
  • Does visibility feel fair across users with different network sizes?
  • Do people feel more supported, less isolated, and safer sharing sensitive information?
  • Are accessibility controls easy to find, understand, and adjust?

What this shows

What this project taught me

The research showed me that inequity in these platforms isn't abstract. It shows up as specific mechanisms: engagement-ranked visibility, storytelling pressure, network effects. Each one can be countered in the design, but only if you name it first. SupportMe is where I learned to treat fairness as a design requirement, not a value statement.

SupportMe was speculative from the start. It answers a real gap in how crowdfunding treats older adults, but no organization commissioned it, and there is no engineering team, no company, and no funding behind it. It exists as a design argument: here is how peer support could work if someone built it. That framing is deliberate. The value is the research insight and the design system underneath it, not a shipped URL.

The biggest unvalidated assumption is whether older adults would trust a peer-matching system run by a platform they have never heard of. Testing that means borrowing trust rather than asking for it: partnering with an AARP chapter, a community center, or a senior center with an existing digital presence, then putting the concept in front of real users in that context instead of in isolation. For an MVP I'd cut it down to peer matching alone and drop the fundraising component entirely. Trying to solve discovery and funding at the same time is exactly what makes the existing platforms overwhelming. One problem first.
